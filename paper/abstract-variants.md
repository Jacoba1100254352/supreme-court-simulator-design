## CELS 2026 Working Paper Abstract

Emergency orders can resolve constitutional disputes before ordinary merits review, making procedure, reasons, and merits follow-through central to court legitimacy. This paper uses a shared-world simulation to compare emergency constitutional-review reforms inside broader court-design bundles. The model creates a filed universe of constitutional disputes, routes filings through review paths and admission filters, and evaluates court designs under ordinary and adversarial political conditions. Across twenty-seven court designs and twenty assumption cases, written reasons, automatic merits follow-through, and no-relief-without-merits rules reduce emergency irregularity more directly than broad appointment or structural reforms, while rights, compliance, process legitimacy, conflict, and administrative-cost tradeoffs remain visible. The results are simulation diagnostics under stated assumptions, not estimates of real-world causal effects.

## APSA Law and Courts, 5000-Character Proposal Stub

How do emergency-review rules shape constitutional court outcomes when they operate inside broader institutional bundles? Court reform debates often isolate single rules, such as appointment method, court size, tenure, voting threshold, emergency procedure, or legislative override. This paper develops a computational model that compares these rules as interacting institutional bundles while centering emergency constitutional review. The model creates a filed universe of constitutional disputes, routes filings through review paths and admission filters, and evaluates court designs under shared docket and political assumptions. It represents appointment methods, tenure, removal, recusal, panel and en banc review, emergency orders, cross-checking courts, constitutional councils, overrides, and post-decision compliance or defiance.

The paper contributes a design-search framework for judicial politics and comparative constitutional law. It asks which emergency-review rules alter tradeoffs among legal stability, rights protection, emergency irregularity, partisan alignment, legitimacy, constitutional conflict, and democratic responsiveness. The current campaign compares twenty-seven institutional designs across twenty assumption cases, including ordinary conditions and adversarial stress cases involving appointment timing, emergency application flooding, override evasion, recusal pressure, and court-expansion retaliation.

The results show that emergency procedure is the clearest modeled lever. Written emergency reasons, automatic merits follow-through, and no-relief-without-merits rules reduce emergency irregularity more directly than appointment or structural reforms, while cross-checking, judicial-electorate selection, and panel designs mainly shift legitimacy, alignment, access, correction, administrative-cost, or conflict diagnostics. The project offers a transparent way to compare constitutional-review institutions while keeping rights, compliance, legitimacy, and implementation tradeoffs in view.
