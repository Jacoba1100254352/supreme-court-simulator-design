## CELS 2026 Working Paper Abstract

Constitutional review institutions are often compared one reform at a time: appointment method, tenure, court size, emergency procedure, voting threshold, override rule, or admissibility gate. This paper introduces a calibrated simulation framework for comparing these reforms as institutional bundles. The model creates a filed universe of constitutional disputes, routes filings through review paths and admission filters, and evaluates court designs under ordinary and adversarial political conditions. It reports direct outputs such as rights-claimant success, public confidence, lower-court compliance, doctrinal depth, emergency grant rates, and domain-specific claimant outcomes separately from derived indices. The current campaign compares fourteen court designs across twenty assumption cases, including appointment capture, emergency flooding, weak mandates, override evasion, and court-expansion pressure. The results do not estimate a causal effect of any real reform. They show which institutional bundles shift tradeoffs among legal stability, rights protection, shadow-docket abuse, partisan alignment, legitimacy, constitutional conflict, and democratic responsiveness, and they identify where additional empirical calibration would most improve constitutional-review design analysis.

## ICON-S / Public Law Abstract, 250 Words

Constitutional review institutions must protect constitutional commitments without becoming unreviewable partisan veto points. This paper develops a computational design-search model for comparing constitutional-review institutions across common docket and political assumptions. The model represents appointment systems, court size, tenure, removal, recusal, emergency procedure, voting thresholds, panel and en banc review, cross-checking courts, constitutional councils, legislative overrides, review-path admissibility, and post-decision political response. It creates a filed universe of constitutional disputes and routes filings through institutional access paths before merits review. The current campaign compares fourteen designs across ordinary and adversarial political environments. The results do not identify an optimal court or predict any particular jurisdiction. They clarify how design bundles shift tradeoffs among stability, rights protection, emergency-docket abuse, partisan alignment, legitimacy, constitutional conflict, and democratic responsiveness. The paper argues that constitutional design debates should evaluate institutional bundles rather than isolated reforms, because the same rule can have different consequences depending on appointment structure, emergency review, admission filtering, and override design.

## APSA Law and Courts, 5000-Character Proposal Stub

How do institutional design bundles shape constitutional review outcomes? Court reform debates often isolate single rules, such as appointment method, court size, tenure, voting threshold, emergency procedure, or legislative override. This paper develops a computational model that compares these rules as interacting institutional bundles. The model creates a filed universe of constitutional disputes, routes filings through review paths and admission filters, and evaluates court designs under shared docket and political assumptions. It represents appointment methods, tenure, removal, recusal, panel and en banc review, emergency orders, cross-checking courts, constitutional councils, overrides, and post-decision compliance or defiance.

The paper contributes a design-search framework for judicial politics and comparative constitutional law. It does not claim to forecast any real supreme court or estimate causal effects from observed reforms. Instead, it asks which bundles of rules alter tradeoffs among legal stability, rights protection, emergency-docket abuse, partisan alignment, legitimacy, constitutional conflict, and democratic responsiveness. The current campaign compares fourteen institutional designs across twenty assumption cases, including ordinary conditions and adversarial stress cases involving appointment timing, emergency application flooding, override evasion, recusal pressure, and court-expansion retaliation.

The results show that design bundles matter. Term regularization and emergency-relief restraints reduce shadow-docket abuse and conflict in the current calibration, while cross-checking and dual-court designs reduce partisan alignment but increase administrative cost and constitutional conflict under the current disagreement-resolution model. The paper also reports direct outputs, such as domain-specific rights-claimant success, public confidence, lower-court compliance, doctrinal depth, emergency grant rates, and override behavior, separately from derived summary indices. The project therefore offers a transparent way to compare constitutional-review institutions without pretending that simulation alone can settle constitutional design.
