## CELS 2026 Working Paper Abstract

Emergency orders can resolve constitutional disputes before ordinary merits review, making procedure, reasons, and merits follow-through central to court legitimacy. This paper uses a shared-world simulation to compare emergency constitutional-review reforms inside broader court-design bundles. The model creates a filed universe of constitutional disputes, routes filings through review paths and admission filters, and evaluates court designs under ordinary and adversarial political conditions. It reports direct outputs such as rights-claimant success, modeled public-legitimacy proxy, lower-court compliance, precedent durability, government noncompliance, enforcement capacity, lower-court resistance, emergency downstream effects, doctrinal depth, emergency grant rates, and domain-specific claimant outcomes separately from derived indices. The current campaign compares twenty-seven court designs across twenty assumption cases, including appointment capture, emergency flooding, weak mandates, override evasion, and court-expansion pressure. The results do not estimate a causal effect of any real reform. They show that emergency-process reforms such as written reasons, automatic merits follow-through, and no-relief-without-merits rules reduce modeled emergency-process irregularity more directly than broad appointment or structural reforms, while still requiring rights, compliance, legitimacy, and administrative-cost tradeoff analysis.

## APSA Law and Courts, 5000-Character Proposal Stub

How do emergency-review rules shape constitutional court outcomes when they operate inside broader institutional bundles? Court reform debates often isolate single rules, such as appointment method, court size, tenure, voting threshold, emergency procedure, or legislative override. This paper develops a computational model that compares these rules as interacting institutional bundles while centering emergency constitutional review. The model creates a filed universe of constitutional disputes, routes filings through review paths and admission filters, and evaluates court designs under shared docket and political assumptions. It represents appointment methods, tenure, removal, recusal, panel and en banc review, emergency orders, cross-checking courts, constitutional councils, overrides, and post-decision compliance or defiance.

The paper contributes a design-search framework for judicial politics and comparative constitutional law. It does not claim to forecast any real supreme court or estimate causal effects from observed reforms. Instead, it asks which emergency-review rules alter tradeoffs among legal stability, rights protection, emergency-process irregularity, partisan alignment, legitimacy, constitutional conflict, and democratic responsiveness. The current campaign compares twenty-seven institutional designs across twenty assumption cases, including ordinary conditions and adversarial stress cases involving appointment timing, emergency application flooding, override evasion, recusal pressure, and court-expansion retaliation.

The results show that emergency procedure is the clearest modeled lever. Written emergency reasons, automatic merits follow-through, and no-relief-without-merits rules reduce emergency-process irregularity more directly than appointment or structural reforms, while cross-checking, judicial-electorate selection, and panel designs mainly shift legitimacy, alignment, access, correction, administrative-cost, or conflict diagnostics. The paper also reports direct outputs, such as domain-specific rights-claimant success, modeled public-legitimacy proxy, lower-court compliance, precedent durability, lower-court resistance, enforcement capacity, government noncompliance, emergency downstream effects, doctrinal depth, emergency grant rates, and override behavior, separately from derived summary indices. The project therefore offers a transparent way to compare constitutional-review institutions without pretending that simulation alone can settle constitutional design.
